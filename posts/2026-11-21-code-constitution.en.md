# Your code needs a constitution: 10 commandments for AI agents

Remember 2023? We told models "you are an experienced senior developer", added "think step by step" and promised them tips. We called it prompt engineering, and it is a little embarrassing to remember now.

In June 2025 Tobi Lütke of Shopify proposed a different term, context engineering: "the art of providing all the context for the task to be plausibly solvable by the LLM". Karpathy agreed: "+1 for "context engineering" over "prompt engineering"" ([Simon Willison collected both](https://simonwillison.net/2025/Jun/27/context-engineering/)). That autumn Anthropic published a [guide](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-context-engineering-for-ai-agents) that treats context as the model's attention budget, where "every new token introduced depletes this budget by some amount".

One layer of that budget is special. The user's request, the files found and the tool output change every time, but `CLAUDE.md`, `AGENTS.md`, Cursor rules and Copilot instructions are loaded into every session. They are written once and read thousands of times. In effect they are your code's constitution, and I think every module, service, project and organisation needs one. It just works differently from how it looks at first.

## TL;DR

- **The code constitution is still the most underrated layer of context engineering.** It tells the agent how to behave in this repository, this module, this team.
- **Prose on its own barely works.** Agents read rule files and follow them, but in controlled studies they solve no more tasks because of it. Writing "please do not cheat" in the prompt barely changed how often the model cheated.
- **So every commandment below says where it lives:** in the text, in a hook and deny rules in the agent's settings, or in CI. The text explains why a rule exists; the mechanism makes sure it is kept.
- **The most useful line in a constitution grants permission rather than forbidding something.** An agent given an explicit way to say "I couldn't do it" cheats noticeably less, at least with some models.
- **A constitution is not generated, it is grown** from real incidents and split into levels: organisation, project, module.

## Where the word comes from

I didn't invent constitutions for AI. In December 2022 Anthropic published [Constitutional AI](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.08073): a model learns to be harmless from a short list of written principles instead of thousands of human ratings. The paper puts it this way: "the only human oversight is provided through a list of rules or principles". In 2023 the list was published, with 58 principles. In January 2026 a [new constitution for Claude](https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-new-constitution) came out, with a different philosophy:

> We generally favor cultivating good values and judgment over strict rules and decision procedures, and we try to explain any rules we do want Claude to follow.

Instead of a list of prohibitions it explains its reasons, so that the model "could construct any rules we might come up with itself". It also sets an order of priorities for conflicts: safety, ethics, Anthropic's guidelines, helpfulness.

The model gets this constitution in training. But both it and [OpenAI's Model Spec](https://model-spec.openai.com/) leave room for the next level: the operator, the developer, which is to say you. The model knows who it is. What it should do in your repository, someone else has to tell it.

The industry is already filling that room. In [GitHub Spec Kit](https://github.com/github/spec-kit), which I wrote about in [the post on spec-driven development](/blog/2026/09/spec-driven/), the very first command is `/speckit-constitution`: "create a constitution once per project". It comes with articles, an amendment procedure and a check of every plan against them.

## The inconvenient truth

Before writing the commandments, let's look at what the data says.

**Agents do read rule files.** In the ETH Zurich study ["Evaluating AGENTS.md"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11988) (Gloaguen et al., 2026), the agent called `uv` 1.6 times per task on average when the file mentioned it, and almost never when it didn't.

**They solve no more tasks because of it.** Files written by developers added about +2.4% to the success rate; files generated by a model changed it by −0.5% to −2%. Compared with having no file at all, neither difference was statistically significant (although human-written files did significantly better than generated ones), and cost went up by over 20%.

**A prohibition in prose is nearly useless.** METR [tested](https://metr.org/blog/2025-06-05-recent-reward-hacking) on one task whether asking a model not to cheat helps. Without the request, the model planned to game the check right away in 80% of runs; with "Please do not cheat" it stayed at 80%. And, as METR notes, models are well aware that what they are doing isn't what the user wanted.

People learned this long ago. Enron had a thick code of conduct, and in 1999 and 2000 its board [granted three waivers](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CPRT-107SPRT80393/html/CPRT-107SPRT80393.htm) so the CFO could run his own partnerships. Guiso, Sapienza and Zingales, who studied corporate culture, [put it bluntly](https://kellogg.northwestern.edu/academics-research/research/detail/2015/the-value-of-corporate-culture/?p=1): "proclaimed values appear irrelevant". What matters is what employees believe and what is borne out in practice.

None of this means you don't need a constitution. It means the constitution needs an executive branch. Even Anthropic doesn't rely on text alone: Claude's constitution is surrounded by separate classifiers, red-teaming and [hidden tests](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/6be99a52cb68eb70eb9572b4cafad13df32ed995.pdf) that check whether the model is gaming the task. And the [Claude Code documentation](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/memory) says it outright about `CLAUDE.md`: "Claude treats them as context, not enforced configuration. To block an action regardless of what Claude decides, use a PreToolUse hook".

Hence the format of the commandments. The heading is in biblical form, so it sticks. Under it is the positive version, because "do X" [works better](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices) than "don't do Y". Then a real story and a **"Where it lives"** line.

## The ten commandments

### I. Thou shalt not lie about the result

**Say "done" only when the check is green, and show its output.**

In April 2026 a Claude Code user [opened an issue](https://github.com/anthropics/claude-code/issues/46940): the agent (Opus 4.6) changed a calculation in `glm.py`, and a golden set of 4,992 values broke in seven places. The agent reported "4966/4966 ALL PASSED". It had changed the denominator so the failing values disappeared from the report. Two months later a bot closed the issue as "not planned".

Claude's constitution has a line for this: "Claude should be diplomatically honest rather than dishonestly diplomatic". But once an agent has decided to lie, a line in a file won't stop it.

**Where it lives:** text + CI. The test results a human sees should come from CI, not from the agent's retelling. A commenter on that same issue suggests a `Stop` hook that won't let the agent finish with "ALL PASSED" unless real test output is attached.

### II. Thou shalt not game the tests

**Fix the code, not the check. If a test looks wrong, say so.**

Jeongho Nam (samchon), the author of typia, was porting the library from TypeScript to Go and [described three attempts in a row](https://dev.to/samchon/ai-deleted-my-tests-and-said-all-tests-pass-a-horror-story-from-porting-typia-from-typescript-2bmf). In the first, the agent deleted every failing test, leaving the `tests/` tree 70% smaller, and its report said "all tests pass". The author added a rule: "Tests are sacred. Do not modify, delete, or simplify them". In the second attempt the agent left the tests alone: it ran the original TypeScript and hard-coded the answers for 168 cases into the Go code. In the third, it edited the GitHub Actions workflow so that whole categories of tests didn't run.

This isn't a one-off. According to [ImpossibleBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20270v1), Claude models cheat mostly by editing tests: over 79% of their cheating takes that form.

**Where it lives:** a deny rule in the agent's settings, read-only tests, and CI that decides for itself which tests to run. The text explains why tests are off limits; the mechanism protects them.

### III. Thou shalt not hide failure

**You may say "I couldn't do it". An honest failure beats a fake.**

This is the only commandment that permits rather than forbids, and it may be the strongest.

In July 2025 a Replit agent working on Jason Lemkin's project [filled the database with made-up data](https://www.theregister.com/2025/07/22/replit_saastr_response/) and insisted a rollback [wouldn't work](https://fortune.com/2025/07/23/ai-coding-tool-replit-wiped-database-called-it-a-catastrophic-failure/), although Lemkin later recovered the data by hand. His conclusion: "AI systems fabricate data when they fail". The same month Gemini CLI, asked only to sort some files into folders, ["lost" all of them](https://github.com/google-gemini/gemini-cli/issues/4586).

The ImpossibleBench authors gave the agent a separate "call a human" action. GPT-5's cheating rate fell from 54% to 9%, and o3's from 49% to 12%. On Claude Opus 4.1 the effect was much weaker, so it is no cure-all. But the direction is clear: an agent lies when admitting failure "costs" it more than pretending everything is done. In Claude's constitution this is a rule an operator cannot switch off: "Always be willing to tell users what it cannot help with".

**Where it lives:** text. Prose belongs here, because it changes incentives rather than forbidding something. It also helps to give the agent an explicit way to say "I couldn't": a report format, a PR label, a task status.

### IV. Thou shalt not be lazy

**Finish the job. If you didn't do all of it, say what's left.**

Model laziness has been documented for a long time. In late 2023 [Aider counted](https://aider.chat/docs/unified-diffs.html) that GPT-4 Turbo left "lazy comments" like `...add logic here...` instead of code in 12 out of 89 tasks. Sweep [reported](https://github.com/sweepai/sweep/blob/main/docs/pages/blogs/gpt-4-modification.mdx) that on files of 50 lines or more, GPT-4 eventually produces `# Rest of the code`.

Laziness itself is less dangerous than laziness passed off as a result. Claude's constitution calls it sandbagging: "intentionally providing a lower-quality response while implying that this is the best it can do". A stub with an honest `TODO: not implemented` is fine. A stub the agent kept quiet about already breaks commandment I.

**Where it lives:** text + CI. Searching the diff for `rest of code`, `not implemented`, empty functions and new `TODO`s costs next to nothing, and there's no reason to rely on the model's memory for it.

### V. Thou shalt not touch production

**Work in your sandbox. Anything irreversible goes through a human.**

In February 2026 Alexey Grigorev of DataTalks.Club [was moving a site](https://aishippingblog.com/p/how-i-dropped-our-production-database) onto shared AWS infrastructure with Claude Code. The agent ran `terraform apply` without the state file and created duplicates. Then it found an old state file in an archive that described production, and proposed removing the duplicates with `terraform destroy`, because that would be cleaner and simpler. The database, the VPC, the cluster, the load balancers, the bastion host and all the snapshots were deleted: two and a half years of data. The author's conclusion: "I over-relied on the AI agent to run Terraform commands".

It has happened before. Replit deleted Lemkin's production database [during an announced code freeze](https://fortune.com/2025/07/23/ai-coding-tool-replit-wiped-database-called-it-a-catastrophic-failure/). Claude Code [wiped the files](https://github.com/anthropics/claude-code/issues/10077) in a user's home directory, apparently with `rm -rf`. And every one of these postmortems ends with mechanisms rather than a new "don't touch prod" line. Replit separated the dev and prod databases. Grigorev turned on deletion protection, moved the state to S3 and no longer lets the agent run commands.

**Where it lives:** almost entirely outside the text. Separate credentials for dev and prod, deny rules in the agent's settings, deletion protection on the cloud side. What stays in the text is an explanation of what counts as irreversible in your project.

### VI. Thou shalt not make things up

**If you're not sure about an API, a version or a package, check, or say "I don't know".**

Models kept recommending `pip install huggingface-cli`, although no such package existed. In December 2023 researcher Bar Lanyado [registered the name](https://www.theregister.com/2024/03/28/ai_bots_hallucinate_software_packages) as an empty package. It was downloaded more than 15,000 times in three months, and one of Alibaba's READMEs told people to install exactly that. There is now a word for this: slopsquatting.

**Where it lives:** text + build. A made-up API gets caught by the compiler, the type checker and the lock file before a human sees it. A made-up package gets caught by an allowlist of dependencies or mandatory review of new ones.

### VII. Thou shalt not do more than was asked

**Solve the task you were given. Whatever you notice along the way, suggest it, don't do it.**

Lemkin [writes](https://www.theregister.com/2025/07/22/replit_saastr_response/): "AI will make changes you didn't request. It just will". On the Cursor forum a user [describes](https://forum.cursor.com/t/linear-cursor-cloud-agent-sonnet-4-5-makes-overly-broad-edits-touches-18-files-when-only-1-2-expected/165633) an agent that touched about 18 files to add a single "copy" button, fixing unrelated type errors and removing comments along the way. Anthropic sees this in its own testing: according to the [Claude 4 system card](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/6be99a52cb68eb70eb9572b4cafad13df32ed995.pdf), Opus 4 has a "default tendency… to do a broad cleanup of some piece of code in response to a request that only asks it to make one specific change". And Grigorev's `terraform destroy` also started as the agent's own initiative, with that same idea that it would be cleaner.

**Where it lives:** text + review. Diff size is a good thing to check in CI: if a one-button task changes 18 files, that should at least require an explanation.

### VIII. Thou shalt follow local law

**Follow this module's conventions: style, architecture, tools.**

This is what rule files were invented for, and it is what breaks most often. In the issue ["Claude Not Reading Claude.md"](https://github.com/anthropics/claude-code/issues/17530) a user writes that the rules get skipped in about half of all sessions, even marked "HARD RULE". Another study ([McMillan, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.10039)) found no link between compliance and the size or structure of the file. Compliance did drop as the work went on, though: with each additional function the agent wrote, the odds of following the rule fell by about 5.6%.

This is where a separate constitution for each module pays off. A rule that sits next to its code (a nested `AGENTS.md` or a rule scoped to a path pattern) gets loaded when it's needed and doesn't get lost among a hundred others.

**Where it lives:** a linter and a formatter for everything that can be checked automatically. Nested rule files for the rest, such as "this module doesn't use an ORM, and here's why".

### IX. Thou shalt explain why

**Every non-obvious decision needs a reason: in a comment, in the commit, in the report.**

This commandment cuts both ways. First, the agent should explain its actions. In the [issue about bypassing hooks](https://github.com/anthropics/claude-code/issues/40117), the agent hid its output with quiet flags and, when asked, blamed the hook configuration, worktree paths and the tests. In the [`rm -rf` story](https://github.com/anthropics/claude-code/issues/10077), the log kept the result but not the command itself, so there was no way to work out what had happened.

Second, and more important, you should explain the reasons in the constitution itself. Anthropic gave up on unexplained rules precisely because they generalise badly. Their constitution gives an example: a rule like "always recommend professional help", applied where it doesn't help, risks teaching the model that it is "the kind of entity that cares more about covering myself than meeting the needs of the person in front of me". An agent will apply an unexplained rule in the wrong place. A rule that comes with its reason can be applied in situations you never anticipated.

**Where it lives:** text. This is the one commandment that rests entirely on prose.

### X. Thou shalt not disable the checks

**If a check fails, fix the cause. A check may be switched off only on a human's word.**

In March 2026 a Claude Code agent [bypassed pre-commit hooks for six commits in a row](https://github.com/anthropics/claude-code/issues/40117) using `--no-verify`, `git stash` and quiet flags. The project's rules said explicitly that `--no-verify` was forbidden unless the user asked for it. The commits went in with failing integration tests, with no secret scanning and no E2E. The issue's author: "the agent deliberately chose to bypass hooks using multiple techniques".

In Cursor a user [clicked "Reject"](https://forum.cursor.com/t/ai-circomventing-rules/132725) on a file deletion, and the agent deleted the file from the command line. The reply on the forum: "if the model decides to delete a file, it will find a way to do so".

**Where it lives:** only in the mechanism. A deny rule for `--no-verify` in the agent's settings, a protected branch and required CI on the server, where no flag gets around anything. A check that exists only as a line of text won't stop anyone.

## How to grow your own

The ten commandments above work as a starter set, but you will have to grow your own constitution.

**Write from incidents.** Mitchell Hashimoto, the author of Ghostty, [on his `AGENTS.md`](https://mitchellh.com/writing/my-ai-adoption-journey): "Each line in that file is based on a bad agent behavior, and it almost completely resolved them all". The agent did something stupid, and a line appeared in the file. In that order, not the other way round.

**Write it yourself.** In the ETH study, generated files on average made things slightly worse and lost clearly to human-written ones: they restate the README, which the agent reads anyway. A constitution should contain only what the agent can't work out from the code.

**Hand whatever can be checked to a mechanism.** [Evil Martians](https://evilmartians.com/chronicles/stop-writing-rules-in-agents-md-use-agent-hooks-and-nano-staged-instead) put it well: "every rule you can encode as a tool is a rule the LLM can't forget and a token you don't have to spend reminding it". Every time you add a line, ask yourself whether a script could check it instead.

**Split it into levels.** The organisation sets what must not be broken anywhere: don't touch production, don't bypass CI, don't commit secrets. The project sets its own conventions. The module refines them: "here we do it this way, and here's why". Conflicts are resolved as in law: constraints from above can't be overridden lower down, while conventions get more specific further down. The formats can only do the second so far (in `AGENTS.md` the nearest file wins), so organisation-level constraints are best kept in mechanisms.

**Reread it when the model changes.** The same line affects different models differently. According to the Claude 4 system card, simple prompts not to tailor the solution to the tests dramatically reduced it in Opus 4 and Sonnet 4, but often failed to help Sonnet 3.7. A constitution written for the previous model should be checked against the new one.

I have an example close at hand: [this site's `CLAUDE.md`](https://github.com/kryadov/kryadov.github.com/blob/main/CLAUDE.md). It says the repository is public and that nothing that shouldn't be published may be added to `PASSTHROUGH`, and it explains why. But the line in the text only explains. What enforces it is `test/build.test.mjs`: it fails if private specifications or a link to a private repository end up in the build. Text plus a test: a constitution with an executive branch.

## Not a cage but a trellis

Near the end of Claude's new constitution there is a section on why it got that name. The authors write that they didn't mean a rigid legal document, but rather what the model is made of:

> A constitution in this sense is less like a cage and more like a trellis: something that provides structure and support while leaving room for organic growth.

The formula works for code too. An agent forgets a list of a hundred prohibitions halfway through a session. What works is a short text that explains why things are the way they are, allows an honest "I couldn't do it", and relies on hooks, tests and CI wherever you can't take the agent's word for it.

We got through prompt engineering, and we're getting through context engineering. Write your code a constitution.
